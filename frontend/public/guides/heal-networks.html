Heal Networks

Defense in Quantum Chess isn’t holding ground. It’s regrowing what your opponent tears off.

Written and maintained by Jasper Taylor for Pura Viba LLC · Updated July 30, 2026

The same contact that zaps an enemy heals a friend. When your piece completes a move, every friendly piece it touches regains its cheapest missing identity — Pawn first, then Knight, Bishop, Rook, Queen, and finally King. On its own that sounds minor. Arranged deliberately, it turns your army into something that repairs itself faster than it can be picked apart. Players who lose slowly are usually the ones who never built a heal network; players who grind wins from equal positions almost always did.

What a heal restores

A heal hands back the cheapest identity the piece is currently missing, re-blurring it upward from the bottom. A piece stripped down to a bare Knight can be healed back toward Knight-or-Pawn ambiguity; a partially collapsed attacker can be topped back up until it’s hard to evaluate again. Two rules shape what actually comes back:

  • Promoted pieces never get Pawn back. Once a piece has promoted it wears its bar for life, and heals skip Pawn for it — they restore the next identity up instead.
  • Claimed identities overflow upward. If the cheapest missing type is fully accounted for elsewhere in the census, the heal can’t double-book it, so it overflows to the next available identity. This is what makes late-game heals dangerous.

Structure beats reaction

You don’t heal by reacting to damage; you heal by building a shape where quiet moves repair someone automatically. Think of a pawn-chain-like cluster in which each piece touches a neighbor. Now every ordinary developing move inside that cluster brushes a friend and tops it back up. The army re-blurs as a side effect of just playing. Against an opponent trying to strip your pieces one zap at a time, a mutual-contact structure means they’re bailing water out of a boat that keeps refilling.

Heal your zappers

The best pieces to heal are often your own attackers. Recall that a piece touches as its cheapest self, so to zap with reach you had to collapse it — and a fully collapsed Knight is honest but readable and cheap to capture. Between attacks, brush that half-collapsed attacker with a friend to top it back into ambiguity. Now it still threatens, but it’s expensive to take and hard to evaluate. Cycling attackers between “collapsed and firing” and “healed and murky” is an advanced habit worth building.

Aim the spillover

Overflow is not a nuisance — it’s a weapon you aim by controlling the census. Early on, cheap slots are open, so heals just hand back pawns. But as pawns get captured (each capture spends an enemy or friendly Pawn slot) and identities get confirmed, the cheap rungs fill up. Now the same heal overflows higher. With both Knights already accounted for, healing a bare piece can make it Pawn-or-Bishop. Deep in a game a single well-timed heal can restore Queen — or, if your side has lost every King possibility, heal King back onto a friendly piece, because the census demands the set contain its King somewhere.

The King-restoring heal

That last case deserves emphasis, because it saves games. If some collapse or zap sequence temporarily leaves you with no King possibility, you are not lost — play continues, and Heal tries King first when the royal identity is missing. A later touch can put King back onto one of your pieces, and conservation may immediately reveal which piece that is. Knowing this exists changes how boldly you can let your own pieces collapse.

Watch the fifty-move clock

One caution: pure heal-shuffling makes no information progress, and the fifty-move draw rule counts progress as any net loss of possibilities anywhere. An army that only ever re-blurs, never collapsing anything, is walking toward a draw in a position it might be winning. Heal to survive and to set up overflow — but keep making real progress with zaps and captures, or a won game drifts into a split point.
