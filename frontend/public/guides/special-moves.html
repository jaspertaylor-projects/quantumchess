Special Moves: Castling, En Passant & Promotion

The three classical exceptions, rebuilt for a board where pieces don’t have fixed identities.

Written and maintained by Jasper Taylor for Pura Viba LLC · Updated July 30, 2026

Castling, en passant, and promotion all survive on the quantum board — but each had to be reinterpreted for a world where a piece is a set of possibilities rather than a known type. Each becomes more flexible and a little stranger. Understanding them turns three “special cases” into real tools.

Quantum castling

Classical castling needs your king and a rook on their original squares, neither having moved. On the quantum board there is no such memory and no privileged square, so castling is redefined by possibility: it applies to any two pieces on your back rank whose lists still include both Rook and King. No move history required.

The two pieces move simultaneously toward each other along a clear path and finish on the two most central empty squares between them. Afterward, both are exactly Rook-or-King — the move measures them into that pair. Two things to plan around:

  • You may castle only once per game. It’s a one-time resource, not a repeatable maneuver, so spend it where the payoff is real.
  • It hands you two Rook-or-King pieces in a single move. That keeps the royal identity flexible — useful while you’re defending a wide royal spread and preparing the revealed-King endgame.

En passant, from any rank

En passant also generalizes. When an enemy piece makes a two-square straight first-move advance while Pawn is still among its possibilities — and because no square is privileged, that advance can come from any rank — you may capture it en passant on your very next turn, exactly as in classical chess: your capturer lands on the square the enemy skipped over.

The measurement cuts both ways. The captured piece collapses to a Pawn (that’s what made it en-passant-able), and so does your capturing piece — only a pawn captures en passant, so performing the capture confirms your piece as a Pawn too. It’s a genuine trade of information: you remove an enemy possibility set, but you spend your own piece’s mystery to do it. The window is one move only, as always.

Promotion as a branch

Promotion is the subtlest of the three, because a piece reaching the far rank might not have been a Pawn at all. So promotion isn’t an assertion — it’s a branch. A piece that still includes Pawn and reaches the far rank promotes immediately, but only in the worlds where it was a Pawn:

  • In the Pawn worlds, it becomes your chosen Knight, Bishop, Rook, or Queen, funded by a Pawn slot in the census.
  • In the worlds where it was never a Pawn, nothing happened — those possibilities carry on unchanged.

A promoted piece wears a solid bar beneath it for the rest of its life. Heals never return Pawn to it. And yes, nine queens are perfectly legal — eight of them are pawns in disguise. The strangest consequence: a promotion can snap back. If your other pieces are later confirmed to be all eight pawns, the census proves this piece was never a Pawn, the promotion branch dies, and the piece reverts to its surviving non-Pawn identities. Promotion is a bet the census can later settle either way.

Using them well

  • Hold your castle until royal flexibility genuinely matters — often as you enter the endgame, not in the opening.
  • Watch for enemy two-square advances by pieces that might be pawns; a one-move en passant window can strip a possibility set you couldn’t otherwise reach.
  • Treat a promotion as conditional. It’s powerful, but don’t build a whole plan on a queen that the census might later prove was never a pawn.
